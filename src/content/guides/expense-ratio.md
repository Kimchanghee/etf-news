## Where do you find the number that matters?

Start with the current prospectus for the exact fund and share class, not a search-result summary. Its standardized fee table separates annual operating expenses from any shareholder fees. In the SEC staff's July 2025 fees bulletin, the expense ratio is the total annual fund operating expenses expressed as a percentage of average net assets. Management fees are one component, not necessarily the whole total. Other expenses can include custody, legal work, accounting and administration.

A website may emphasize the management fee because it is a familiar headline. Check whether the prospectus total is different. Also check the document's date and any supplements. A number copied from an old factsheet can be accurate for that document and still be unsuitable for a current comparison. Record the fee figure, document date and whether it is gross or net alongside the ticker.

## What does 0.20% mean in dollars?

Convert the percentage before calculating: 0.20 divided by 100 equals 0.002. On a hypothetical constant $10,000 balance, $10,000 multiplied by 0.002 is $20 for one year. On $50,000, the same approximation is $100. This is a way to understand scale, not a reconstruction of the fund's actual accounting. The balance changes with market prices, distributions, subscriptions and withdrawals, and the disclosed ratio relates to average assets.

The fund pays operating expenses from its assets. You do not ordinarily see a separate year-end $20 withdrawal from your brokerage cash balance for this example. Instead, the effect is embedded in the value and performance of the investment. When comparing published returns, first read their methodology. Do not automatically subtract the expense ratio again from a return that already reflects operating expenses.

## Why can small annual differences matter?

Consider two purely hypothetical investments starting at $10,000, with zero gross growth and constant annual fees of 0.20% and 0.50%. Under a simplified annual deduction model, their values after one year are $9,980 and $9,950. After ten years they are about $9,801.79 and $9,511.10. The roughly $290.69 difference comes from repeated fee deductions and the changing base, not a prediction of ETF performance.

With positive investment returns, money removed for fees also loses the opportunity to participate in later growth. Negative markets do not make fees disappear. Our [cost calculators](/tools/) let you change assumptions rather than pretend a single return path is inevitable. Actual funds do not deduct one identical invoice on the final day of each year, so keep model output separate from a statement of actual realized expenses.

## What changes when there is a fee waiver?

A prospectus may show an expense ratio before and after a waiver or reimbursement. The lower figure can reflect an agreement to reduce expenses temporarily or indefinitely. The SEC bulletin warns that some waived expenses may be recouped later. Read the footnote for duration, termination conditions and any reimbursement provisions rather than treating the lower number as permanent.

For a useful comparison, write down both figures. If one fund has a temporary net fee and another has a stable contractual arrangement, comparing only today's advertised percentages hides a relevant difference. You do not need to guess which waiver will end. You can instead calculate a scenario at the current net fee and a second at the gross fee, clearly labeling both as assumptions rather than forecasts.

## Which costs sit outside the expense ratio?

The expense ratio is not a complete receipt for investing. The SEC fees bulletin identifies brokerage commissions and changes in premiums or discounts as ETF transaction considerations. It also explains that some indirect costs, such as underlying portfolio transaction costs, may not appear in the expense ratio. Advisory or account charges can sit at the investor level too. A commission-free order therefore is not proof of a cost-free transaction.

For an ETF purchase, inspect the [bid-ask spread](/guides/spread-liquidity/) separately. For a holding-period comparison, examine [tracking difference](/guides/tracking-difference-error/) without assuming every difference is an additional fee. Fund returns already reflect many operating effects. Adding the stated fee to the full historical underperformance can double-count part of the same economic drag. Taxes also depend on the investor and account, so this guide does not assign a universal tax cost.

## How should two ETFs be compared fairly?

First ask whether they provide comparable exposures. A cheap broad equity fund and a more expensive specialized bond fund are not substitutes merely because both trade on an exchange. Confirm the objective, benchmark, strategy, portfolio risks and currency. Only then compare operating costs, trading conditions and the relevant historical return record. Lower fees do not erase market risk or guarantee a better realized outcome between different portfolios.

Create a short comparison note: exact fund identity; prospectus and supplement dates; total gross expense ratio; net ratio and waiver terms; other investor charges; intended trade size; and planned holding period. Leave missing fields visibly unresolved instead of entering zero. This keeps a comparison auditable and prevents a tiny fee difference from becoming a false precision exercise. The goal is to understand which costs repeat, which occur when trading, and which depend on circumstances you have not yet established.
