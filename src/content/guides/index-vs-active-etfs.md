## Compare the assignment before comparing the score

An index ETF and an active ETF can own similar securities while following different instructions. An index strategy seeks approximately the return of a specified benchmark. An active strategy lets the adviser select investments without having to conform to an index. The SEC staff's April 2025 comparison explains that both ETFs and mutual funds can use either approach. Exchange trading is a wrapper feature, not proof of passive management.

Write the objective in one sentence before comparing expenses or historical returns. Is the fund tracking a broad stock market, a bond segment, or one industry? Does it seek income or capital appreciation? If the objectives differ, a return ranking may mostly reflect which market segment happened to do well. It may tell you very little about the quality of implementation.

## An index is a set of choices

An index is not the market itself. It is a defined basket governed by inclusion and weighting rules. A narrow sector portfolio is not automatically diversified across the economy. A long list of holdings can still assign large weights to a few companies. The number of positions and the distribution of their weights answer different questions and should be recorded separately.

Read the named benchmark and the fund's explanation of its implementation. The SEC bulletin distinguishes buying all index securities from representative sampling. Sampling is not necessarily a failure to follow the objective: it may be the disclosed method. But it means the fund and benchmark portfolios need not be identical. Do not assume every constituent is held in exactly the index weight.

Index rules may also screen for characteristics such as size or financial measures. The useful question is which exposures those rules produce, not whether the passive label sounds reassuring. Obtain the applicable methodology and its date. An appealing index name is not a complete description of its selection rules, concentration or possible changes.

## Active discretion does not guarantee a result

Active management permits decisions within a prospectus mandate. That freedom can produce different weights, cash balances and security selections from a comparison index. It does not establish that the manager will anticipate turning points correctly. A fund can follow its process and still lose money or underperform. Historical success is evidence about a past period, not a future contractual return.

Read what the manager may do as well as what the fund currently owns. A holdings snapshot describes one observation date; the mandate describes permitted behavior. If they appear inconsistent, look for supplements and explanatory reports before drawing conclusions. A temporary allocation is not necessarily a permanent change of objective, and a changed portfolio does not by itself prove misconduct.

## Compare actual costs rather than stereotypes

The index or active label cannot replace the fee table. Both incur expenses that reduce returns. Record total annual operating expenses, any waiver terms and the document date. Separate those charges from brokerage costs and the bid-ask spread encountered when trading. Commission-free trading can still involve a spread. A low management fee may also differ from the total operating expense ratio.

For a hypothetical scale comparison, 0.15% of a constant $20,000 is $30 a year. At 0.60%, the equivalent approximation is $120, a difference of $90. These are not real fund quotes. They do not determine which strategy will produce the greater net return. Actual expenses depend on changing assets and fund accounting, so the simple multiplication is only an illustration.

Published returns may already include operating expenses. Subtracting the expense ratio again would double count that cost. Our [expense-ratio guide](/guides/expense-ratio/) explains this boundary, while the [spread guide](/guides/spread-liquidity/) separates annual expenses from transaction friction. Keep the return convention beside each number rather than assuming every source uses identical definitions.

## Make the performance comparison fair

Use matching dates, currency and distribution treatment. Comparing a price return against a total return can create a false advantage. For an index fund, distinguish the return gap from its benchmark from variability in that gap. These are separate questions, discussed in our [tracking guide](/guides/tracking-difference-error/). For an active fund, the selected comparison benchmark must also represent a meaningful opportunity set.

Consider a broad stock fund and a concentrated technology fund. If technology rises faster during the period, the concentrated portfolio may lead regardless of whether it is active or indexed. Calling that result proof that one management style wins confuses exposure with implementation. State the limitations rather than forcing unlike funds into a simple league table.

## Keep a mandate worksheet

Record the exact fund identity, objective, benchmark, implementation approach, principal risks and cost components. Beside each item, retain the source and document date. Separate explicitly disclosed facts from your interpretation. If a benchmark or exposure cannot be reconciled, leave that field unresolved instead of choosing a convenient substitute.

Finally, identify what would make the comparison stale: a revised mandate, changed index, altered waiver, different manager or materially changed holdings. That produces a repeatable review rather than a one-time opinion. This is general education, not investment advice or a recommendation to prefer either strategy. No current fund return or future outcome has been verified here.
