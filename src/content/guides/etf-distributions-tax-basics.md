## Define the tax scope before reading the yield

This guide describes selected U.S. federal income-tax concepts for individual investors holding conventional fund shares in taxable accounts. It draws on the inspected 2025 edition of IRS Publication 550 and the SEC's fund comparison. It does not calculate a tax bill, provide filing instructions for every situation, or establish rules for another country. Future tax-year instructions and personal circumstances must be checked separately.

Retirement accounts, nonresident investors, state taxes, foreign taxes and products with different legal structures can require different analysis. A commodity partnership or exchange-traded note should not be assigned a conventional ETF's treatment just because it trades beside one. Establish the exact product and account type before applying any general statement about ETF distributions.

## Cash received is not the same as profit

A distribution transfers value from the fund to shareholders. The cash amount alone does not establish how much the investment earned. Evaluate changes in investment value together with distributions under a stated total-return convention. A high payout percentage can coexist with a falling share price. Do not interpret a distribution rate as a guaranteed future return or evidence that principal is protected.

A fund may distribute different types of income or gains, and the final tax classification matters. Marketing descriptions such as monthly income do not replace the tax documents. Keep payment dates and amounts, but reconcile them with the final information supplied by the fund or broker. An estimated character during the year may not be the final reporting character.

## Ordinary and qualified dividends differ

Publication 550 explains that ordinary dividends are generally ordinary income, not capital gains. Form 1099-DIV reports ordinary dividends in box 1a. A qualified-dividend amount is part of ordinary dividends that can be eligible for capital-gain tax rates when the applicable requirements are met. It is not an additional distribution to add a second time.

Eligibility includes holding-period and other conditions. A number in the qualified-dividend box is not permission to ignore your own holding period. The IRS publication includes examples where the investor's short holding period prevents otherwise identified dividends from receiving qualified treatment. This guide does not reproduce a complete eligibility decision tree or assume that every equity ETF distribution qualifies.

## Capital-gain distributions are a separate category

The IRS explains that capital-gain distributions from mutual funds and other regulated investment companies are generally reported as long-term capital gains regardless of how long the investor held the fund shares. Form 1099-DIV identifies these in box 2a. This is different from the gain or loss when you personally sell shares, whose treatment depends on your own transaction and applicable rules.

Consequently, buying a fund shortly before a distribution does not establish that the payment will be treated as your own short-term trading gain. Conversely, a distribution does not erase a loss on the shares. Keep the fund's distribution record and your sale record distinct. Specific short-holding-period loss rules and other exceptions may require additional review.

## Return of capital changes basis

A nondividend distribution is generally a return of capital rather than current earnings and profits. Publication 550 says it reduces the basis of the investment until basis is recovered. Once basis reaches zero, additional nondividend distributions are capital gains, with holding-period treatment determined under the applicable rules. Calling return of capital tax-free without this boundary hides a future consequence.

For a hypothetical single lot with $1,000 basis, a $120 nondividend distribution leaves $880 basis, assuming no other adjustments. If a later qualifying nondividend distribution is $900, the first $880 reduces basis to zero and the remaining $20 is capital gain. The example demonstrates basis arithmetic, not a forecast or a determination of the applicable rate.

## Reinvestment does not make reporting disappear

Choosing additional shares instead of cash generally does not remove taxable distribution reporting in a taxable account. Publication 550 also explains that distributions used to buy new shares establish the cost basis of those shares. Keep acquisition dates, quantities and reinvested amounts. Otherwise, later sale calculations can omit basis and make the reported gain appear larger than it should be.

For a simple example, a $50 taxable distribution reinvested in additional shares can still be reportable income while creating $50 of initial basis in the newly acquired shares. That does not mean the same $50 is immediately taxed twice. Future sale proceeds are compared with adjusted basis under the relevant rules. Special adjustments and account arrangements can complicate this illustration.

## ETF efficiency is not a blanket exemption

The SEC explains that many ETFs use in-kind exchanges and historically have fewer capital-gain distributions than similar mutual funds. That structural tendency does not promise zero distributions or zero tax. Investors can still owe tax on distributions and on gains from selling shares. Nor does the observation establish a personal advantage inside a tax-advantaged account.

## A year-end record check

Retain information returns, transaction confirmations, basis records and corrected forms. Ask the issuer about an incorrect information return instead of silently substituting an estimate. IRS Publication 550 notes that taxable dividends may still need reporting even without a received Form 1099-DIV. For the actual tax year, use current instructions and qualified assistance where needed. This is education, not investment or personalized tax advice.
